What Is Peptide Laboratory Testing?

Peptide laboratory testing is the group of analytical methods used to verify a synthetic peptide batch. A peptide is a short chain of amino acids made by solid-phase synthesis, then cleaved, purified and freeze-dried. Each of those steps can leave behind errors: truncated sequences, deletion sequences, residual solvents, salts or microbial contamination.

A proper test panel answers four questions:

  • Identity: is it the correct molecule?
  • Purity: how much of the material is the correct sequence?
  • Content: how much of the powder is actually peptide?
  • Safety: is it free from endotoxin, microbes and toxic residues?
In short: a clean, independent laboratory report turns a label claim into verifiable evidence.

Purity is not the same as content

A report showing 98% HPLC purity does not mean the vial holds 98% peptide by weight. Lyophilised peptides normally contain water and counter-ions, so net peptide content is often well below 100%. Always look at both numbers.

Why identity needs its own test

HPLC shows that a sample is mostly one substance, not which substance. Mass spectrometry measures molecular weight and is the standard way to confirm identity.

How to Read a Certificate of Analysis (CoA)

A Certificate of Analysis is the summary document for a batch. Use this checklist when reviewing one:

  1. Laboratory details: a named lab with address and contact information.
  2. Batch or lot number: it must match the product in your hands.
  3. Test dates: recent and consistent with the manufacturing date.
  4. Methods listed: HPLC conditions, MS type, and any compendial references.
  5. Raw data: the actual chromatogram and mass spectrum, not just a table of results.
  6. Specification limits: pass or fail criteria stated next to each result.
Red flags: no batch number, a screenshot instead of a report, identical reports for different lots, missing chromatograms, or a laboratory that cannot be contacted. When in doubt, ask the laboratory to verify the report directly.

Testing Example: Semaglutide

Large peptides show why thorough analysis matters. Semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist peptide with a molecular weight of roughly 4,113.6 Da and a fatty-acid side chain attached to the backbone. That modification makes it more complex to synthesise and to analyse than a simple linear peptide.

For a semaglutide batch, a laboratory would typically check:

  • Identity: mass spectrometry matched against the expected molecular weight.
  • Purity profile: HPLC separating the main peak from related substances.
  • Content: net peptide content, not only chromatographic purity.
  • Safety tests: endotoxin and sterility for material intended for injection.

How a Peptide Sample Moves Through the Lab

  1. Sample receipt: logging, labelling and chain of custody.
  2. Preparation: accurate weighing and dissolution in a suitable solvent.
  3. Separation and detection: HPLC run, followed by mass spectrometry.
  4. Safety panel: endotoxin, sterility and residual testing where required.
  5. Review and report: a second analyst checks data before the CoA is issued.

Look for laboratories working under quality systems such as ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation or GMP, since these require validated methods and traceable records.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is peptide laboratory testing?

It is a set of analytical tests, mainly HPLC for purity and mass spectrometry for identity, used to confirm that a peptide is the right molecule, at the stated purity and content, and free from harmful contaminants.

What does 98% purity on a peptide CoA mean?

It usually means that 98% of the UV-detected peak area in an HPLC run belongs to the target peptide. It does not mean the vial contains 98% peptide by weight, because water and counter-ions are also present.

What is the difference between purity and net peptide content?

Purity describes how much of the peptide material is the correct sequence. Net peptide content describes how much of the total powder weight is peptide at all, as opposed to water, salts and counter-ions such as TFA or acetate.

Why is mass spectrometry needed if HPLC already shows a clean peak?

HPLC shows that a sample is mostly one substance but not which substance it is. Mass spectrometry measures molecular weight and confirms identity.

What is endotoxin testing?

Endotoxin testing, typically the LAL assay, measures bacterial endotoxin contamination. It matters for any material intended for injection.

How can I check that a Certificate of Analysis is genuine?

Check for a named laboratory with contact details, a batch or lot number matching the vial, test dates, the method used, and the raw chromatogram and mass spectrum. Ask the laboratory directly to verify the report.
